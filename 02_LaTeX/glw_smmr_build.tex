\begin{abstract}
Routine molecular monitoring in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) produces
data that violate the assumptions of standard survival and landmark
analyses: log-transformed minimal residual disease (log-MRD) is measured
at irregular clinic visits, is left-censored at an assay reporting floor,
and the endpoint of deep molecular response (DMR) is detected only at the
discrete visits at which a patient is tested. We develop a Bayesian joint
framework that models the latent MRD trajectory and the DMR event process
coherently while respecting these features. A longitudinal sub-model
represents patient-specific MRD dynamics with left-censoring for
assay-floor observations; an interval sub-model links the latent
trajectory to first documented DMR. To resolve the circularity created by
defining the endpoint through the same biomarker used as its predictor, we
reformulate DMR onset as a latent threshold-crossing event and derive its
interval-observed likelihood. We further develop dynamic prediction with
horizon-specific recalibration and an informative visit-process extension.
In a real-world cohort of 87 patients (495 MRD observations, 275 at-risk
intervals), the joint model was computationally stable, calibrated at the
interval level, and outperformed Kaplan--Meier, interval-only, landmark,
and exact-floor alternatives by Brier score, aligning molecular-response
modelling with the clinical observation process.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} joint longitudinal--survival models;
interval-censored events; left-censored biomarkers; latent
threshold-crossing; dynamic prediction and recalibration; Bayesian
hierarchical modelling; chronic myeloid leukemia; deep molecular response.

\section{Introduction}

Tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy has transformed the prognosis of
chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), shifting long-term care toward sustained
molecular control, adherence, adverse-effect management, and the possible
selection of patients for treatment-free remission
\cite{Hochhaus2020ELN,Jabbour2025JAMAReview,Haddad2026Algorithms,Senapati2023CMLManagement}.
Deep and durable molecular response, including MR4.5 and deep molecular
response (DMR), is central to long-term response assessment and to
discussions about treatment discontinuation
\cite{Hughes2014EarlyMolecularResponse,Rasekh2026TFRPredictor,Ono2026MR45,Takahashi2026JSKI}.
Consequently, statistical models for CML are increasingly asked to turn
longitudinal molecular histories into individualized, calibrated
statements about response.

Routine molecular monitoring data, however, rarely have the structure
assumed by simple survival or prediction models. Three features recur.
First, measurements are obtained at \emph{irregular} clinical visits, so
the exact time at which a patient first attains DMR is never observed;
only the interval between two visits that brackets the transition is
known. Treating first \emph{observed} DMR as an exact event time ignores
this interval observation and confounds biological onset with visit
timing. Second, deep molecular values are frequently reported at an assay
or reporting floor. Treating floor values as exact numerical measurements
understates uncertainty below the reporting limit; the correct
information is that the true burden lies at or below the floor. Third, and
more subtly, the clinical endpoint is \emph{defined} by a threshold on the
same biomarker that any joint model uses as a predictor: DMR is
$\text{log-MRD} \le -4.5$. Using the latent biomarker to predict a
separately coded DMR ``event'' can therefore be circular.

Joint longitudinal--survival models provide a natural framework for
connecting repeated biomarkers with event processes
\cite{Wulfsohn1997JointModel,Rizopoulos2016JMbayes}, and recent
methodological work has extended them to complex observation schemes and
flexible biomarker--event associations
\cite{Lovblom2026MixedObservationJointModel,Niu2026QuantileJointModel}.
What is missing for molecular monitoring is a single framework that
simultaneously (i) handles assay-floor left-censoring in the biomarker,
(ii) respects the interval nature of endpoint detection under irregular
visits, and (iii) removes endpoint circularity by defining the event
directly through the latent biomarker trajectory, while (iv) delivering
calibrated, individualized dynamic predictions without overstating
clinical decision utility.

This paper develops such a framework and illustrates it on a real-world
CML cohort. Our contributions are fourfold. We specify a Bayesian joint
longitudinal--interval model in which patient-specific MRD trajectories
with left-censored assay-floor observations drive an interval-hazard model
for first documented DMR (Sections~\ref{sec:long}--\ref{sec:interval}). We
introduce a \emph{latent threshold-crossing} formulation that defines DMR
onset as the first time the latent MRD trajectory crosses the clinical
threshold, deriving both a deterministic and a probabilistic
interval-observed likelihood that resolve endpoint circularity
(Section~\ref{sec:threshold}). We develop dynamic prediction with
horizon-specific logistic recalibration
(Section~\ref{sec:dynamic}) and an informative visit-process extension
that models monitoring intensity as a function of the latent trajectory
(Section~\ref{sec:visit}). Finally, we specify a simulation design to
evaluate the framework under irregular visits, floor censoring, and
informative monitoring (Section~\ref{sec:sim}). The joint
longitudinal--interval model is fitted to the cohort and assessed by
posterior predictive checks, calibration, model comparison, and dynamic
prediction (Section~\ref{sec:application}); the threshold-crossing,
informative-visit, and simulation components are developed here as
methodology, with their empirical evaluation and external validation the
subject of ongoing work.

\section{Motivating application: a CML molecular-monitoring cohort}
\label{sec:motivating}

We motivate and illustrate the methodology with a real-world CML
monitoring cohort. The raw visit-level file contained 504 molecular
records on 89 patients. After removing records with missing or invalid
essential modelling fields, the longitudinal analysis file contained 495
complete molecular observations from 87 patients; the corresponding
patient-level file contained 87 records and 275 at-risk intervals for
first documented DMR (Table~\ref{tab:cleaning-audit}). Analysis files used coded
identifiers; direct identifiers were held only in a private linkage file.

\input{table_03_data_cleaning_audit.tex}

DMR was defined as $\text{log-MRD}\le-4.5$ and complete molecular response
(CMR) as $\text{log-MRD}\le-5.0$; both indicators were audited by
recomputation from the processed values. A structural feature of the data
is central to the modelling: 239 of 495 observations (48.3\%) were at or
below the log-MRD floor of $-5.0$, and no retained observation fell in the
interval $(-5.0,-4.5]$. As a result, DMR-positive and CMR-positive counts
coincide in the retained data --- not through a coding error, but because
all DMR-positive values were reported at the $-5.0$ assay floor. This
directly motivates left-censored modelling of floor observations and
limits the ability of the data alone to distinguish DMR from deeper
response. Treatment time was recorded in months for clinical summaries and
converted to years for all likelihood components; if $t^{(m)}$ denotes
months since imatinib initiation, the model uses $t=t^{(m)}/12$, with
time effects on the $\log(1+t)$ scale.

Monitoring was heterogeneous, with median follow-up of 39.0 months and a
median of 5 visits per patient. The median visit gap was 6.0 months;
50.3\% of gaps exceeded 6 months and 20.0\% exceeded 12 months. This
irregularity, together with the fact that DMR is documented only at
visits, is precisely what the interval and threshold-crossing likelihoods
below are designed to accommodate.

\section{Statistical framework}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Longitudinal sub-model with assay-floor left-censoring}
\label{sec:long}

Let $m_i(t)$ denote the latent biological log-MRD trajectory for patient
$i$ at treatment time $t$ (years):
\[
m_i(t)=\beta_0+\beta_1\log(1+t)+\beta_2\{\log(1+t)\}^2
+b_{0i}+b_{1i}\log(1+t),
\]
with independent patient-specific random effects
\[
b_{0i}\sim N(0,\tau_0^2), \qquad b_{1i}\sim N(0,\tau_1^2).
\]
An earlier specification included a random intercept--slope correlation;
because it was weakly identified in this cohort, the final model treats the
random effects as independent, which improved computational stability
without materially changing the fit. The observation-scale mean includes a
sample-source adjustment for bone-marrow versus peripheral-blood assays:
\[
\mu_{ij}=m_i(t_{ij})+\beta_{\mathrm{BM}}\,I(\mathrm{bone\ marrow}_{ij}).
\]
For observations above the reporting floor,
$y_{ij}\sim N(\mu_{ij},\sigma_y^2)$. For observations reported at the
assay floor $c_F=-5.0$, the likelihood contribution is left-censored,
carrying the information that the true value lies at or below the floor:
\[
\Pr(y_{ij}^{\ast}\le c_F)=
\Phi\!\left\{\frac{c_F-\mu_{ij}}{\sigma_y}\right\}.
\]

\subsection{Interval sub-model for first documented DMR}
\label{sec:interval}

Because DMR is detected only at monitoring visits, the event is
interval-observed. For each patient the first at-risk interval ending in a
DMR-positive measurement is coded as the event interval, and preceding
intervals as at risk without event; patients without documented DMR by
last follow-up are right-censored. Let $L_{ik}$ and $R_{ik}$ be the start
and end of at-risk interval $k$, with $\Delta_{ik}=R_{ik}-L_{ik}$ and
$t_{ik}^{mid}=(L_{ik}+R_{ik})/2$. The interval hazard links the latent
trajectory to DMR:
\[
h_{ik}^{\mathrm{DMR}}=
\exp\{\gamma_0+\gamma_1\log(1+t_{ik}^{mid})
+\gamma_2\log(1+\Delta_{ik})
+\alpha\, m_i(t_{ik}^{mid})\},
\qquad
p_{ik}^{\mathrm{DMR}}=
1-\exp\{-h_{ik}^{\mathrm{DMR}}\Delta_{ik}\}.
\]
The gap term $\gamma_2\log(1+\Delta_{ik})$ absorbs the mechanical
dependence of documentation probability on interval length, and the shared
$m_i(\cdot)$ couples the two sub-models so that lower latent MRD raises the
probability of documented DMR.

\subsection{Latent threshold-crossing formulation}
\label{sec:threshold}

The interval-hazard model treats DMR as an event with a hazard driven by
the biomarker, but DMR is \emph{defined} by the biomarker crossing $-4.5$.
To represent the endpoint directly and remove this circularity, we define
DMR onset as a latent threshold-crossing time,
\[
T_i^{\mathrm{DMR}}=\inf\{t\ge 0:m_i(t)\le c_{\mathrm{DMR}}\},
\qquad c_{\mathrm{DMR}}=-4.5.
\]
Because molecular response is observed only at clinic visits, first DMR
remains interval-observed. Writing $M_i(t)=\min_{0\le u\le t}m_i(u)$ for
the running minimum of the latent trajectory, a patient with documented
first DMR in $(L_i,R_i]$ contributes the deterministic crossing event
\[
M_i(L_i)>c_{\mathrm{DMR}}, \qquad M_i(R_i)\le c_{\mathrm{DMR}},
\]
and a censored patient contributes $M_i(C_i)>c_{\mathrm{DMR}}$. These hard
indicators are non-smooth; for gradient-based Hamiltonian Monte Carlo they
are replaced by a probabilistic threshold-crossing model that additionally
accommodates uncertainty in the clinical cut-point. Letting the effective
patient-level threshold be $C_i\sim N(c_{\mathrm{DMR}},\sigma_{\mathrm{thr}}^2)$,
the likelihood contribution for an observed DMR interval is
\[
\Phi\!\left(\frac{M_i(L_i)-c_{\mathrm{DMR}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{thr}}}\right)
-
\Phi\!\left(\frac{M_i(R_i)-c_{\mathrm{DMR}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{thr}}}\right),
\]
and the censoring contribution is
$\Phi\{(M_i(C_i)-c_{\mathrm{DMR}})/\sigma_{\mathrm{thr}}\}$. As
$\sigma_{\mathrm{thr}}\to 0$ the probabilistic likelihood recovers the
deterministic crossing event. This formulation makes the modelled endpoint
identical to the clinical definition and handles irregular monitoring by
observing the crossing only through inter-visit intervals.

\subsection{Dynamic prediction and recalibration}
\label{sec:dynamic}

For clinical use the framework produces individualized dynamic
predictions. At landmark time $s$ the target is
\[
\Pr(T_i^{\mathrm{DMR}}\le s+H\mid T_i^{\mathrm{DMR}}>s,\mathcal H_i(s)),
\]
where $\mathcal H_i(s)$ is the MRD history available up to $s$, with
landmarks $s\in\{6,12,18,24\}$ months and horizons $H\in\{6,12,24\}$
months. Because absolute probabilities from a mechanistic joint model can
be miscalibrated, we apply horizon-specific logistic recalibration,
\[
\mathrm{logit}\{p_{i,\mathrm{cal}}(s,H)\}
=a_H+b_H\,\mathrm{logit}\{p_{i,\mathrm{orig}}(s,H)\},
\]
with the recalibration layer assessed by bootstrap optimism correction.
Strict prospective prediction requires that patient-specific random
effects be updated using only $\mathcal H_i(s)$; the internal analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:application} conditions on the full longitudinal record
and is therefore an apparent (upper-bound) assessment of calibration,
which we treat accordingly.

\subsection{Informative visit-process extension}
\label{sec:visit}

Monitoring intensity may itself depend on molecular response: a patient
doing well may be seen less often, and more frequent testing yields more
opportunities to document DMR. To assess sensitivity to informative
visiting, we model the visit intensity jointly with the trajectory,
\[
\lambda_i^V(t)=
\exp\{\delta_0+\delta_1[m_i(t)+4.5]+\delta_2\log(1+t)+u_i\},
\qquad u_i\sim N(0,\sigma_u^2),
\]
so that visit intensity depends on the latent MRD, time, and a
patient-level monitoring random effect $u_i$. For visit times
$v_{i1},\ldots,v_{iM_i}$ in $[E_i,C_i]$ the visit-process likelihood is
\[
L_i^V=
\left\{\prod_{\ell=1}^{M_i}\lambda_i^V(v_{i\ell})\right\}
\exp\left\{-\int_{E_i}^{C_i}\lambda_i^V(s)\,ds\right\},
\]
with the cumulative intensity evaluated by quadrature. Because this
extension adds weakly identified parameters in a cohort of 87 patients, it
is presented as a sensitivity model to be evaluated primarily by
simulation and in larger cohorts.

\subsection{Priors, estimation, and computation}
\label{sec:computation}

Estimation is fully Bayesian via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo in Stan.
Weakly informative priors were fixed before fitting:
\[
\beta_0\sim N(-2.5,2^2),\quad
\beta_1\sim N(-1,1^2),\quad
\beta_2\sim N(0,0.5^2),\quad
\beta_{\mathrm{BM}}\sim N(0,1^2),
\]
\[
\sigma_y\sim \mathrm{Exponential}(1),\quad
\tau_0,\tau_1\sim \mathrm{Exponential}(1),\quad
\gamma_0\sim N(-2,2^2),\quad
\gamma_1,\gamma_2\sim N(0,1^2),\quad
\alpha\sim N(-0.5,0.75^2).
\]
Convergence was assessed by $\widehat{R}$, bulk and tail effective sample
size, divergent-transition counts, tree depth, and E-BFMI. In the latent
threshold-crossing and informative-visit models, the running-minimum and
hard-indicator terms are implemented through smooth approximations to
preserve differentiability.

\subsection{Model assessment and comparison}
\label{sec:assessment}

Model adequacy was evaluated with posterior predictive checks for the
longitudinal component (predictive coverage; observed versus predicted
assay-floor rate) and with calibration of DMR probabilities at both the
interval and patient level (calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope,
and grouped calibration plots). Discrimination and overall accuracy were
summarized by Brier scores. The joint model was compared against four
simpler alternatives that a practitioner might otherwise use: a
descriptive Kaplan--Meier analysis of first observed DMR; an interval-only
model with timing and visit-gap terms; a landmark MRD model; and a joint
model that treats floor observations as exact $-5.0$. Refittable simpler
models were internally validated by patient-cluster bootstrap with
optimism correction; for the Bayesian joint models, fixed-posterior
bootstrap summaries are reported.

\subsection{Simulation design}
\label{sec:sim}

To characterize the operating properties of the framework beyond the
single cohort, we specify a simulation study evaluating whether the
interval-hazard model, the deterministic and probabilistic
threshold-crossing models, and the informative visit-process extension
recover DMR onset and calibrated DMR probabilities. Data-generating
scenarios cross non-informative versus informative monitoring, exact
versus floor-censored molecular values, threshold uncertainty around
$-4.5$, sparse versus dense monitoring, and sample sizes of 87, 150, and
300 patients; performance is measured by bias, coverage, calibration, and
Brier score. This design fixes the evaluation protocol used in the ongoing
extension of this work.

\subsection{Fitted joint model}

Computation was stable: no divergent transitions, maximum tree depth 7,
minimum approximate E-BFMI 0.610, maximum $\widehat{R}$ 1.003, minimum
bulk effective sample size 1359, and minimum tail effective sample size
1395; no parameter had $\widehat{R}>1.01$ or effective sample size below
400. Posterior summaries are given in
Table~\ref{tab:joint-model-posterior}. The latent time effect was strongly negative
($\beta_{\mathrm{time}}=-3.561$; 5th--95th posterior percentiles $-4.351$
to $-2.778$), indicating marked decline in latent log-MRD over follow-up,
with a positive quadratic log-time term ($\beta_{\mathrm{time}^2}=0.502$;
0.078 to 0.914) consistent with nonlinear rather than linear dynamics. The
association parameter was negative
($\alpha_{\mathrm{MRD}}=-1.275$; $-1.724$ to $-0.899$), so lower latent MRD
was associated with higher interval probability of documented DMR. These
are monitoring-oriented associations, not externally validated
treatment-decision thresholds.

\subsection{Posterior predictive checks and calibration}

Among non-floor observations, 90\% posterior predictive coverage was 0.957
and 95\% coverage was 0.984; the observed assay-floor rate (0.483) was
close to the posterior mean floor probability (0.414)

\subsection{Model comparison}

The joint model had lower interval-level and patient-level Brier scores
than the descriptive Kaplan--Meier, interval-only, landmark, and
exact-floor alternatives (Table~\ref{tab:model-comparison-validation}). The improvement
over the exact-floor joint model isolates the contribution of
left-censored assay-floor handling, and the improvement over the
interval-only and landmark models isolates the value of coupling the
longitudinal and event processes. This supports the joint framework for
monitoring-oriented inference; it does not by itself establish clinical
decision readiness.

\subsection{Dynamic prediction and recalibration}

The internal dynamic-prediction analysis generated 300 eligible
landmark--horizon records. Before recalibration, the joint model
overpredicted 6-month DMR probability (mean predicted 0.506 versus
observed 0.361; Brier 0.179), modestly overpredicted the 12-month horizon
(0.626 versus 0.576; Brier 0.162), and underpredicted the 24-month horizon
(0.721 versus 0.828; Brier 0.081). Horizon-specific logistic recalibration
aligned mean predicted probabilities with observed rates; bootstrap
optimism-corrected recalibrated Brier scores were 0.167, 0.172, and 0.063
at 6, 12, and 24 months (Table~\ref{tab:dynamic-prediction-validation},
Figure~\ref{fig:dynamic}). Because these predictions used patient-specific
random effects estimated from the full longitudinal record, they are
apparent performance; strict prospective prediction requires posterior
updating restricted to the history available at each landmark.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Principal findings}

We have developed a Bayesian framework that aligns the statistical model
with the structure of routine CML molecular monitoring: repeated MRD
measurements, assay-floor reporting, irregular visits, and
interval-observed DMR. In the motivating cohort, the fitted joint
longitudinal--interval model was computationally stable, close to
calibrated at the interval level, and outperformed Kaplan--Meier,
interval-only, landmark, and exact-floor alternatives by Brier score. The
comparison against the exact-floor joint model shows that treating deep
values as left-censored, rather than as exact $-5.0$ measurements,
measurably improves fit.

\subsection{Methodological contribution}

The central methodological contribution is the latent threshold-crossing
formulation. Because DMR is defined by $\text{log-MRD}\le-4.5$, using the
latent trajectory to predict a separately coded DMR event is partly
circular; defining onset as the first crossing of the threshold by the
latent trajectory makes the modelled and clinical endpoints identical, and
its interval-observed likelihood --- deterministic, or probabilistic with
threshold uncertainty $\sigma_{\mathrm{thr}}$ --- handles irregular
monitoring naturally. The probabilistic version is also better suited to
gradient-based sampling than the non-smooth deterministic indicators. The
identical DMR and CMR counts in the retained data are a consequence of
assay-floor reporting rather than a coding artifact, and reinforce the
need for left-censored modelling of floor observations.

\subsection{Clinical interpretation}

The fitted model should be read as a monitoring-support framework, not a
treatment-decision rule. The negative association between latent MRD and
interval DMR probability is biologically coherent and supports using
serial molecular history for response assessment, but it does not validate
the model for TKI discontinuation, treatment-free-remission eligibility,
or changes in treatment. Dynamic prediction with recalibration improves
calibration-in-the-large and provides individualized horizon
probabilities, but the current estimates are apparent rather than strictly
prospective.

\subsection{Informative monitoring}

Monitoring is irregular and may be informative: testing frequency can
depend on molecular response, adherence, clinician concern, or
institutional practice, and more frequent testing creates more
opportunities to document DMR. The visit-process extension models visit
intensity as a function of the latent trajectory, time, and a
patient-level monitoring effect, providing a route to assess this
sensitivity; because it adds weakly identified parameters in a modest
cohort, it is best evaluated by simulation and in larger data.

\subsection{Limitations}

The cohort was modest (87 patients; complete core covariates in 62) and
came from a single real-world source without an external validation
cohort. Monitoring was irregular, so patients with longer or denser
follow-up had more opportunity to document DMR. The retained data
contained no observations in $(-5.0,-4.5]$, limiting the ability to
distinguish DMR from CMR descriptively. The fitted interval-hazard model
does not by itself remove endpoint circularity; the threshold-crossing
formulation addresses it and is evaluated in ongoing work. The dynamic
predictions used random effects from the full record and are therefore
apparent. The informative-visit and simulation components are specified
here as methodology; their empirical operating characteristics are
established in the simulation study of Section~\ref{sec:sim} as that work
completes.

\subsection{Future work}

Future work will fit and validate the deterministic and probabilistic
threshold-crossing models, compare them with the interval-hazard model,
and quantify whether the threshold-crossing endpoint improves calibration
and interpretability; execute the simulation study of
Section~\ref{sec:sim}; implement strict prospective landmark prediction
with history-restricted posterior updating; and test whether recalibrated
dynamic probabilities transport to independent CML cohorts through external
or temporal validation.

\section{Conclusions}

We have presented a Bayesian framework for CML molecular monitoring that
is aligned with irregular clinical observation, assay-floor reporting, and
interval-observed DMR. The fitted joint model is computationally stable and
supports monitoring-oriented inference, and the latent threshold-crossing
formulation resolves endpoint circularity by defining the event directly
through the latent biomarker trajectory. Dynamic prediction with
recalibration yields individualized horizon probabilities. Clinical
decision use requires prospective calibration and external validation.
